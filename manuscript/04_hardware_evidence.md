## 4. Hardware evidence across the adaptation chain

This section reports what has been measured. It is organised by *what was timed* rather than by tuning technology, because one of the review's findings — that electronic and mechanical ranges overlap — becomes invisible the moment the two are separated into different sections. Before any value can be compared with another, the type of quantity has to be named: element switching, aperture configuration, mechanical element response, mechanical surface response, closed-loop convergence, open-loop replay rate and stabilised-radiation onset all recur here, and each is defined in Supplementary Table S2 with its start event, end event, timed object and the substitution it most often invites. Values may be compared only within a quantity type, and even then only when their objects and architectures match.

### 4.1 Flexible programmable reflective apertures

The flexible intelligent microwave metasurface with shape-guided adaptive programming [6] is the reviewed set's most instructive hardware source, and carries the most complete adaptation measurement available. The aperture is a 480 × 240 mm three-layer flexible sheet of metallic meta-atoms on polyimide, a polydimethylsiloxane substrate and a serpentine-mesh ground, with 32 × 16 meta-atoms at a 15 mm period and varactors driven over 0–30 V. Control granularity is columnar: the 32 columns share bias voltages, so the surface has 32 independently addressable channels rather than 512 independent elements. Measured operation spans 3.0–3.4 GHz with up to approximately 270° of reflection-phase tuning across incidence angles from 0° to 45°, the normalised reflection amplitude staying above 0.4 across all bias and incidence combinations. We report that floor as the source reports it and do not convert it into a radiation or reflection efficiency, since the source does not define it as one and the conversion would need loss accounting the paper does not supply. Shape reconstruction from the 32 strain sensors is validated against a stereo reference at a root-mean-square deviation as low as 2.36 mm at a maximum displacement of 45 mm — an accuracy, not a timing.

Its timing decomposition is the only one of its kind in the reviewed set. Data preparation for the network inputs takes approximately 4 ms, about 2 ms of sensor reception at roughly 1200 Hz per channel plus about 2 ms of processing; inference across 32 channels takes approximately 2 ms; and the bias-voltage supply module responds in 5.25 ms. The components sum to 11.25 ms, a figure the paper's own timing figure annotates beside the measured total of 16.76 ms, the residual of approximately 5.5 ms arising from full-cycle control of all 32 channels over an RS-232 link at 115 200 bps [6, pp. 4–5; Supplementary Note 6]. The methods differ between components, which matters for how much weight each carries: the 4 ms and 2 ms figures are thread-timing averages taken inside the control program, while the 5.25 ms figure is an oscilloscope measurement of the supply module's output following a control signal. The supplement adds that deformation intervals must exceed 16.76 ms for voltage updates to remain synchronised, expressed as reliable operation at deformation frequencies up to 60 Hz, and projects without measuring that under 10 ms would be reachable with faster acquisition circuits and a different communication protocol.

Three properties of the 16.76 ms interval must travel with it whenever it is quoted, and they are the reason it recurs in this manuscript. It begins at shape *acquisition*, so it excludes the deformation that produced the shape. It ends at bias-voltage *supply*, so it excludes any mechanical settling and any interval before the far field is stable. And the deformation is externally imposed: the supplement describes the mechanical platform as a frame carrying two telescopic links and three movable fulcrums, adjusted by hand to set the curvature, with no actuation timing and no closed loop around the shape, so the system compensates for a shape it did not choose. The interval is therefore a compensation latency, and reading it as a FIM adaptation time would misattribute it by at least two stages at each end.

The same supplement reports a second, methodologically independent experiment which, uniquely in the reviewed set, terminates at electromagnetic output rather than at a control signal. A finger-triggered sensor replaces the strain input; a dual-channel oscilloscope records the trigger on one channel and, on the other, received beam power demodulated by a logarithmic detector calibrated over −10 to −60 dBm at 3.1 GHz. The measured delay from trigger to stabilised RF output is 16.7 ms [6, Supplementary Note 6]. This review therefore cannot and does not claim that radio-frequency stabilisation is never timed in this literature. What must travel with the figure is its condition: the geometry is static throughout, and the interval contains no shape change.

What the platform establishes at application level is substantial and should not be understated. Mounted on an aerofoil model — the motivating scenario being aerofoil flutter under inertial, aerodynamic and elastic forces — it maintains a real-time QPSK video link at 3.1 GHz to a receiver 2 m away at 0° or 30°, with error vector magnitude staying around −20 dB while the surface is bent dynamically, and that demonstration is untimed. A second, independent flexible reconfigurable aperture confirms that such apertures are buildable: the conformal reconfigurable reflectarray of polydimethylsiloxane and printed liquid metal reaches 16.13 dBi peak gain with ±45° scanning at 9 GHz [18]. Its curvature is known a priori, it has no shape sensing, and it reports no timing of any kind.

### 4.2 Rigid electronically reconfigurable surfaces: the timing comparators

The scalable integrated RIS [4] is a one-bit PIN-diode surface at 9–10 GHz with 180° ± 20° phase difference, built as 16 × 16 tiles each carrying an Artix-7 FPGA, four tiles forming a 32 × 32 prototype. It reports an update time below 0.1 ms and a tile ON/OFF configuration update below 10 ms. Both are upper bounds rather than measured means, and — contrary to how the pair is easily read — both concern the same object. The abstract attaches the first to "each element", but no per-element measurement appears in the body: the figure traces to an end-to-end reconfiguration time for one 16 × 16 tile, about 40 μs over the Ethernet-based interfaces and up to 100 μs over USB/UART, against an internal FPGA update of about 40 ns once the configuration is stored [4, p. 6]. The second is the tile configuration update as operated in the near-field measurement campaign [4, pp. 8–9]. Both end at the configuration write, neither states an electromagnetic criterion, and as bounds they are mutually consistent. What separates them is the context in which each was stated, not the object or the technology — which is a direct demonstration that a reported latency without its start event, end event and object is not yet a comparable quantity. The same paper reports that the control network uses time-multiplexed latching, reducing the effective update rate to a quarter of the raw parallel speed, and reports what such a panel costs to run — 8.25 W with all diodes off, 13 W with all on, and 11.25–11.60 W during beam steering for the 32 × 32 array. Measured control power is rare in this literature and this is the only such figure in the reviewed set.

Those two bounds are *controller write* times in the sense of Supplementary Table S2: they run from a command issued by the compute element to the control state being written, and they say nothing about how long the tuning device itself takes to change state. The transition of the tuning material is a separate quantity type with its own start and end events, and the values reported for it in the reviewed set are numerically much larger without being measurements of the same thing. The liquid-crystal RIS based on defected delay lines [5] is the reviewed set's most carefully characterised material transition: a 4.6 μm liquid-crystal layer, 12 × 10 unit cells at 62 GHz, 6.8 GHz (10.9 %) bandwidth, insertion loss below 7 dB, and beam steering from −50° to +50°. Its measured response times are τ_on ≈ 15 ms and τ_off = 72 ms, defined between 10 % and 90 % thresholds and strongly asymmetric. A projection of below 2 ms for a 1 μm layer appears in the same discussion and is explicitly not a measurement of the fabricated device. Read as material transitions alone, the values reported in the reviewed set run from approximately 15 ms, measured on the fabricated device, to the tens of seconds the same paper attributes to reflectarray-type liquid-crystal implementations [5, p. 7]; both endpoints are liquid crystal. The semiconductor end of that quantity type carries no figure at all, because no reviewed source measures a diode or varactor transition: the flexible metasurface calls its varactor switching negligibly short and reports no measurement of it [6], and the mechanism survey ranks modulation rates rather than transition times [9]. Nothing here supports an inference that semiconductor transitions are slow, and nothing here places the sub-0.1 ms controller-write bound of [4] on the same scale as the 15 ms material transition of [5]. They are different quantity types, and on each of the two technologies only one of the two has been measured.

### 4.3 Commanded mechanical morphing, without a radio

The filamentary mechanical metasurface [7] achieves reversible out-of-plane deformation of about 30 % of an 18.0 mm sample using distributed Lorentz forces at currents below 27.5 mA in a 224 ± 16 mT field. Element response time is below 0.07 s, measured by monitoring a single serpentine beam with a 60 fps side camera and identifying the frame after which no displacement deviation is visible; system morphing is characterised as within 0.1 s. Its distinguishing feature is closed-loop control, with in-situ stereo imaging driving a gradient-based optimiser.

The cost of that loop is the most instructive number the platform reports, and it is fully visible only in the supplementary information. One function evaluation, comprising a voltage update, a wait for the sample to settle, a stereo capture with processing and a loss evaluation, costs 0.35 ± 0.15 s, of which 0.19 s is imaging and template matching and 0.1 s is a deliberate settling pause. Each optimiser iteration requires 4(N + M) + 2 function evaluations, which is 34 for a 4 × 4 sample, and convergence takes 5 to 15 iterations. The paper states the resulting figure directly: a 4 × 4 sample takes an average of approximately 2.5 minutes to morph into a shape from the zero-actuation initial state [7, Supplementary Note S6, Supplementary Table 1].

The separation between that figure and the platform's sub-0.1 s mechanics is exactly what the comparability classes exist to preserve, and it is large: the element response cited downstream as evidence of feasibility (below 0.07 s) and the demonstrated closed-loop convergence (approximately 150 s) differ by a factor of roughly two thousand, and the reviewed system literature quotes neither. What takes 2.5 minutes is *searching* for the voltages that produce a target shape on a nonlinear structure with a camera in the loop; once found, the same platform replays stored voltage sequences open-loop at a 10 fps update rate. Two things follow, and both are properties of this platform rather than laws. In the one demonstrated shape-control loop in the reviewed set, actuation is not the dominant contributor to loop time; sensing, perception and search are. Whether a radiating FIM would also be perception-limited depends on its sensing modality, its verification criterion and its compute load, none of which has been measured — but a closed-loop FIM's rate may be set by perception rather than by actuation, which is a reason to instrument the sensing path early, and no reviewed system model represents this stage at all. The same reported quantities also invite a substitution: multiplying the feedback-cycle time by the iteration count yields a convergence figure roughly two orders of magnitude below the value the source states directly, because an iteration here is 34 function evaluations rather than one cycle.

The soft shape-programmable surface [8] uses Lorentz-actuated liquid-metal microfluidic ribbons in an elastomer and reports a three-level decomposition that separates the quantities this review has to keep apart: an isolated ribbon responds in approximately 30 ms measured (approximately 50 ms by finite-element analysis); a full surface develops from flat in approximately 300 ms, of which approximately 250 ms is viscoelastic membrane relaxation; and shape-to-shape switching takes approximately 650 ms, being two surface developments plus approximately 50 ms of script processing. The element-to-surface penalty is a factor of ten, and the paper names its cause as the membrane rather than the actuator, which matters because a membrane is a materials property. The two mechanical platforms agree on the sign of that penalty and disagree on its size — a factor of about 1.4 for the filamentary mesh against about ten for the soft surface — and the disagreement is itself informative: the penalty depends on construction, and no reviewed measurement establishes it for a radio-frequency stack.

### 4.4 Flexible active arrays: demonstrated capability, no reported duration

The flexible active antenna array work [20] demonstrates two 256-element, 30 × 30 cm arrays at mass densities around 0.1 g cm⁻² that remain fully functional and programmable at concave and convex bend radii below 23 cm, and deliver approximately 80 mW of DC power wirelessly to a receiver about a metre away; the demonstrated steering and focusing ranges are stated by the authors to be limited by the measurement range rather than by the array. This is the closest any reviewed source comes to the operation a FIM would need after changing shape — re-establishing coherent aperture behaviour on a deformed, large, real radiating surface under closed-loop feedback — and it reports no duration for that operation. One qualification travels with it: this is an active transmitter with per-element RFICs, phase-locked loops and on-chip self-sensing receivers, not a passive reflector, and its calibration strategy depends on exactly that.

Displayed on that axis, one observation about ranges follows, and it qualifies the standard dichotomy. Mechanical movement is generally slower than electronic reconfiguration, as the closest survey states qualitatively [11], and that is a reasonable first approximation which cannot be applied from an architecture label alone. An electronic material transition on one platform (72 ms) is numerically larger than a measured partial electronic chain on another (16.76 ms) and lies within a factor of about four of a full mechanical surface morph on a third (≈300 ms). Those three values were obtained at different frequencies, on different mechanisms, on different objects, with different start and end events, and they sit in three different quantity types; they may be displayed on a common axis to show that their reported ranges overlap, and they must not be treated as like-for-like performance measurements, pooled statistically, or used to infer the bottleneck of an integrated architecture. The qualification that survives is that architecture labels alone do not establish a universal latency ordering, so "fast electronics, slow mechanics" holds only with mechanism-, object- and architecture-specific qualification, and which stage binds must be established for the specific combination of material, actuator, control network and aperture in question.

### 4.6 Coverage of the chain, and where it stops

Three counts follow from the per-source coding in Supplementary Section S6, and they are not interchangeable, so each is stated with the axis it counts. Quantitatively evidenced stages: the maximum is six, reached by [6] and [7]. Measured stage durations: the maximum is four, reached by [7] alone. Stages carrying any duration information, whether measured or apportioned out of an aggregate: the maximum is six, reached by [6].

The platform that measures four stage durations is the filamentary mechanical metasurface [7], which times sensing, geometry estimation, control transmission and morphing, each with its own value and dispersion in its supplementary budget, and which has no radio-frequency layer at all. The flexible microwave metasurface [6] measures three — inference, electronic update, and stabilised radiation in the separate static-geometry experiment — and apportions three more out of its measured compensation-loop total, of which the control-transmission figure is a residual rather than a measurement. It does not command its geometry. Counted on the axis that permits comparison, the two sets overlap at sensing, geometry estimation, optimisation and control transmission, and differ exactly where the question lies: [6] carries the radio-frequency stages and cannot command a shape, while [7] carries the mechanical stages and does not radiate. Neither can be extended into the other by further measurement. Every other source is sparser still: one measured stage duration each for the liquid-crystal panel [5] and the soft shape-programmable surface [8]; none for the scalable integrated RIS [4], whose two reported bounds end at a configuration write rather than at a meta-atom state change and therefore bound an aggregate containing that stage rather than timing it; and none for the flexible active array [20], which demonstrates seven of the ten stages as working operations and attaches a duration to none of them.

Figure 3 shows the pattern by architecture class. Read across it, the stages sit on mutually incompatible platforms: measured morphing durations exist only on A4 platforms with no radio-frequency layer; no platform in the reviewed set measures a settling duration at all, the closest figures being a component identified within an aggregate on one A4 platform and a deliberately allocated wait on the other; electronic state transitions are timed only on A5 rigid panels and one A3 flexible aperture; sensing, inference and control transport on a flexible radiating surface are timed only on an A3 platform where geometry is a disturbance rather than a control variable; and post-deformation calibration on a deformable radiating aperture is demonstrated only on an A7 active transmitter, without a duration there or anywhere else in the reviewed set. Stabilised radiation is the clearest case for keeping the two axes apart: five platforms establish it quantitatively through patterns, gain, scan range, insertion loss or delivered power [6, 18, 5, 4, 20], and the one that attaches a duration to it holds its geometry static [6]. The gap is not that stabilised radiation is unevidenced; it is that its onset after a commanded shape change is unmeasured.

> **Figure 3.** *Adaptation-chain coverage by architecture class.* Rows are platform classes rather than individual papers; each cell records the strongest evidence any platform in that class provides for that stage. Fill colour gives the stage evidence status, and the appended marker gives the separate question of what is known about duration: **·T** a measured stage duration, **·(T)** a duration available only as a share, component or residual of an aggregate, **·[R]** a rate rather than a duration, and no marker untimed. The one cell that would carry an allocated interval — mechanical settling on the commanded-mechanical class — is marked † and carries no measured duration in either case. Several cells are quantitatively measured and carry no timing marker, because a radiation pattern, a gain figure or a delivered power is a measurement but not a timing. *(File: `figures/fig3_adaptation_chain.pdf`.)*

A total could in principle be assembled by taking each stage from wherever it was measured: the electronic loop from A3, plus surface morphing from A4, plus unmeasured settling and calibration terms. Such a sum would concatenate measurements taken on four different physical objects, one of which has no meta-atoms, another of which cannot bend, and none of which has both a controlled geometry and a measured radiation pattern. It would have the appearance of an engineering estimate while adding quantities that are not commensurable. What the decomposition licenses instead is weaker and far more defensible: within the reviewed set, no source times the adaptation chain end to end on any single object, and the reason is architectural rather than incidental.

Six capabilities nonetheless reach prototype-measurement level or higher: large flexible apertures that still radiate; in-situ shape sensing on three platforms; electronic phase reconfiguration timed at the controller; deformation-aware electronic compensation demonstrated over the air; commanded mechanical shape control with closed-loop verification; and conformal design at fixed curvature. None of these constituent capabilities is absent from the reviewed evidence base when considered separately, and separate demonstrations of them do not establish that they cease to constrain the design once combined, since integration changes the mass, the damping, the control granularity and the calibration burden of each. What no reviewed source measures is the intersection, which Section 5 states as the validation gap.
