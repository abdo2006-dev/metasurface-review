## 5. High-mobility feasibility and the validation gap

### 5.1 A stage-resolved answer

Assembling the demand side of Section 3 with the evidence of Section 4, the review question admits a stage-resolved answer instead of a verdict. Table 3 gives it, under one convention that matters. The "must it track fast fading?" column records a consequence of a stated assumption, not a finding, and the assumption is named in the cell. The central question of this review is which stages are exempt, and a table that presupposed its own answer would not be informative.

**Table 3 — Stage-by-stage answer to the review question.** Architecture classes are those of Table 1. Bold text inside a cell marks the answer given in that column and the qualification that limits it.

| Stage | Must it track fast fading? | Governing driver | Best available evidence | Evidence status | What this supports |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **S1** sensing (geometry) | no — tracks *deformation*, not fading | platform dynamics | ≈4 ms strain read [7]; 0.35 ± 0.15 s stereo cycle [22] | duration measured (A4); apportioned share only (A3) | geometry sensing at deformation rates; imaging, not actuation, sets the closed-loop rate on the one closed platform |

Three points stand out when the rows are read together. The fast-timescale demand is established by modelling, not by measurement: channel acquisition is the stage that must track fading, and the evidence for its timescale is simulated and analytical throughout, resting on a correlation window, a Doppler figure and a pilot-slot structure. This review found no measurement of channel acquisition under high mobility with a reconfigurable surface in the loop. The electronic update stage has measured support only on liquid crystal, and no deadline against which to judge any of it; whether a sub-0.1 ms tile configuration bound is *sufficient* would require a stated update deadline for the same state variable, on a comparable architecture, at a specified carrier and mobility, against a stated correlation criterion, and none exists in the reviewed set. The geometry stages are where the evidence changes character: morphing durations are measured only on platforms that do not radiate; no independently measured settling duration exists anywhere in the reviewed set; and calibration has been demonstrated on an object that does radiate, but was not timed.

Stated as an answer to the question posed in Section 1: conditionally on the two-timescale premise, that the shape is commanded from slower statistical or geometric information rather than from instantaneous small-scale fading, the fast, channel-dependent path comprises channel estimation, the optimisation that produces the fast state, the transport of that command to the surface, and the electronic state update. The geometry stages need not track instantaneous fading under that premise. The premise is stated as a premise: it is what the system literature assumes, not what it establishes. Nor is it this review's reconstruction, since the published version of the high-mobility FIM–ISAC study itself offers morphing once over several coherence intervals as an alternative to per-realisation morphing [1, p. 13322]. The support for the fast path is uneven in a way that is architectural: the requirement rests on analysis and simulation, while the hardware capability rests on measurements taken on rigid panels that cannot bend.

### 5.2 How timing values travel

The FIM system papers reviewed here do not claim to have measured their own hardware assumptions, and it would be unreasonable to expect them to. They do, however, rest on assertions about hardware that can be traced to a primary source, and the traceability matters because the substituted quantities are the ones Section 4 shows to be missing.

Two of the three premises are quickly stated. The first is that once surface shapes have been computed for a distinct channel realisation, "the optimized surface shapes can be used irrespective to changes in delays, Doppler shifts and waveform" [1, footnote 10, p. 13327]; traced to source, this is a footnote carrying no validity duration, no channel-statistics condition, no degradation bound, no sensitivity analysis and no experiment, and the paper's own simulations optimise per realisation rather than testing reuse. The second is that FIM surface shapes "are only updated on the timescale of the channel's coherence block" [32], which is cleaner and more testable, and which asserts that command transport, actuation, settling and any recalibration all complete inside one coherence block.

The third premise needs more space. A recent FIM system paper tabulates the "key parameters of existing FIMs", listing morphing periods of 30 ms for the liquid-metal platform, 10 ms for the filamentary platform, and 500 ms for a third, photomechanical platform not held in our corpus, and states in text that "the response time of FIM surface morphing reaches 10 ms according to Table I" [33]. The tracing below was performed against the published version of record, whose Table I and reference list are quoted here as printed. Tracing these values to the primary sources shows that the 30 ms entry corresponds to the liquid-metal platform's measured *isolated-ribbon* response, whereas that paper's measured full-surface development time from flat is approximately 300 ms, and it states shape-to-shape switching as the sum of two such developments and approximately 50 ms of script processing, which is approximately 650 ms [8, pp. 3–4]. An element-level figure has become a surface-level one. We could not locate a corresponding 10 ms value anywhere in the complete primary source package of the filamentary platform, which we obtained and examined in full: the main text, the Extended Data figures, the 71-page supplementary information and the peer-review file. The timing quantities reported there are an element response below 0.07 s, system morphing within 0.1 s, a function-evaluation cycle of 0.35 ± 0.15 s, and closed-loop convergence of approximately 2.5 minutes [22]. A full-text search of the package for "10 ms", "0.01 s", "sub-element", "filament", "transient" and "rise time" returns no occurrence, and the unit "ms" does not appear in the supplementary information at all. The tabulated deformation figures do trace correctly, since the 18 mm aperture and the ratio of about 0.3 match the reported sample size and 30 % out-of-plane deformation, so the entry is drawn from that paper. One further observation bears on it: the element response was measured with a 60 fps side camera, a frame period of approximately 16.7 ms, so the instrument used to obtain the fastest mechanical quantity in the paper could not have resolved an interval of 10 ms. We therefore record this as a discrepancy requiring verification, not as an error: no derivation or source for the value was found in any full text we inspected, and the seven unread studies cannot be spoken for.

Table I introduces a third problem, separate from both of those and of a different kind. Its displayed citation numbers do not resolve to the named hardware papers in that paper's own bibliography. Its column headers attach reference numbers 38, 39 and 40 to Ni, Bai and Niu respectively; the bibliography gives its reference 38 as a wireless-communications textbook, and lists Ni, Bai and Niu as its references 39, 40 and 41. The body prose of the same paper cites all three hardware papers correctly, so it is the table's labels that are displaced. The reported years diverge in the same table: it dates Bai to 2021 and Niu to 2022, while its bibliography records September 2022 and December 2021 respectively, so the two are interchanged. The same subsection later describes the fastest deformation response as 1 ms, against a Table I minimum of 10 ms, and attaches that statement to the reference triple 38, 39 and 40 — consistent with the table's column labels and not with the bibliography, in which the first member is the textbook. **We select none of these readings as authoritative and infer no intent.** The identification of each column is not in doubt, because the material, actuation principle and deformation figures in it match one primary source and no other; what does not resolve is the numbering and the dating attached to them, and the 1 ms figure has no counterpart in any primary source we read, nor in the table it is said to summarise. We therefore record these as unresolved source-level discrepancies. They bear on the traceability of the premise rather than on the physics, and they are the kind of thing the reporting fields of Section 6 are meant to make visible on the page.

The resulting chain runs as follows. A mechanical-metasurface paper measures an element on a substrate with no radio; a system paper tabulates that value as a surface morphing period; a further system paper concludes that the morphing period is comparable to the channel coherence time and therefore that real-time adaptation is feasible; and the architecture is analysed on that basis. At no point in the chain is the substituted quantity measured on an object that radiates.

The reach of the pattern was tested, not assumed. Among the 13 of 20 candidate citing studies whose full texts were accessible, five instances of scope change occur, in four studies [1, 33, 34, 35]; the remaining nine cite the same primary hardware and attach no timing value at all, two of them sharing no author with either the network or the primary sources [12, 36]. Restraint is therefore the majority pattern within the studies we could read, and it is not confined to authors outside the network. These four studies therefore do not constitute four independent confirmations, because they belong to one connected co-authorship network: four different first authors are not four independent groups when common senior authors run through them. Two of the five instances were inserted between an author team's own conference or preprint version and its journal version, a pattern invisible to anyone reading only one version. Seven studies could not be read, and an abstract-level scan establishes nothing here because propagation occurs only in body text, so a further instance cannot be excluded. Every count is a lower bound on a partially observed set, and no proportion, rate or prevalence is estimated from it: this is a documented finding about one collaboration network, not a measurement of how a field reasons. The instance-by-instance inventory, the author-disjointness resolution for both the read and the unread studies, and the full study register are in Supplementary Sections S5 and S8.

Every one of those substitutions is enabled by the same thing: an informal term used where a definition is needed. "Response time", "morphing time", "morphing period" and "switching speed" all name a duration without naming the object it applies to, the event that starts it, or the criterion that ends it. The same failure mode can arise whenever an informal quantity name, an ambiguous unit and a plausible arithmetic step are combined, which is why it is better treated as structural than as a property of any particular author. The remedy follows directly: a timing value reported with its object, its start event and its end event cannot be silently rescoped, because the substitution becomes visible on the page. That is what Section 6 asks for.

### 5.3 What the gap is

The gap is narrower than a reader arriving from the system literature might expect, and misplacing it would misdirect the experimental effort that could close it. It does not lie in any of the six capabilities listed at the end of Section 4, each of which reaches prototype measurement or higher and several of which reach over-the-air demonstration. It lies in their intersection, and it has a precise form: the transition from compensating a shape imposed from outside to commanding a shape and knowing when it has arrived, on an aperture that must simultaneously radiate. Within the reviewed set, every measurement of the commanded-shape side was taken on a platform with no meta-atoms, no bias network and no radio-frequency ground plane, and every measurement of the radiating side was taken on a platform where geometry is exogenous. That is the empty cell of Figure 1, restated as a measurement question.

The four unmeasured intervals deserve individual statement, because their experimental cost differs sharply.

**G1 — commanded actuation time for a radiating aperture.** How long from an actuation command to the intended displacement, on a surface carrying the layers a grounded reflective aperture is built from: meta-atom metallisation, a dielectric substrate and a ground plane. The stack differs for transmissive and active architectures, and the gap is stated here for the grounded reflective case the reviewed flexible platforms exemplify. The nearest available figures, tens of milliseconds at element level and hundreds at surface level, are for bare mechanical substrates, and the one flexible radiating surface in the reviewed set required a serpentine-mesh ground plane simply to permit bending at all [7], with its dynamic properties uncharacterised.

This gap is narrower than it would be if stated against the reviewed set alone, and it is worth stating at its true width. A duration for a geometric change *has* been reported on an aperture carrying meta-atom metallisation, a dielectric substrate and a ground plane: 240 s, for the 4D-printed reflecting surface of Section 3.1, recovering a column spacing from 0 mm to 2 mm [16]. Two things follow. The figure is three orders of magnitude above every mechanical value in Table 2, which is consistent with this review's substantive point rather than against it — moving a real radio-frequency stack is not cheap. And it is not the measurement G1 asks for: it times a thermally driven recovery to a single memorised shape, not an actuation command to a chosen geometry, and it terminates when the structure has recovered rather than at any stated electromagnetic criterion. G1 is therefore stated for the commanded case specifically — a command issued to a selected geometry, timed on an aperture that radiates. We did not locate that measurement inside the reviewed set, and we did not locate it outside.

**G2 — mechanical settling to a phase-relevant tolerance.** No source in the reviewed set measures a settling duration in its own right. The closest figure attributes approximately 250 ms of a 300 ms surface response to viscoelastic membrane relaxation [8] — a component identified within an aggregate rather than measured separately, and it is a materials property of a bare elastomer. At 28 GHz one wavelength is 10.7 mm, so a tolerance stated as a twentieth of a wavelength would be roughly half a millimetre. That is offered to indicate the form such a criterion would take, and not as a value this review recommends. No reviewed source states a settling tolerance of any kind.

**G3 — post-deformation calibration duration.** The operation is demonstrated on a deformed 256-element flexible aperture using closed-loop focusing against a remote receiver [23], and no duration is reported. The alternative architecture, folding the geometry-to-bias mapping into a pre-trained network as the flexible microwave metasurface does, sidesteps the question by making calibration a training-time cost, but that approach is validated for a specific aperture, band and deformation family, and its generalisation cost is unmeasured.

**G4 — onset of trustworthy radiation after a commanded geometry change.** This gap must be stated more narrowly than the other three, because one reviewed source does terminate a measured interval at stabilised radiation. That measurement establishes that the end event is well defined and that it is instrumentable with ordinary laboratory equipment. It does not contain a shape change.

Underneath the timing problem sits one that no amount of stopwatch work would solve. Within the reviewed set, no platform is architecturally positioned to optimise geometry against measured channel state. That assumption requires four things on one object, and Table 4 names them: a radiating aperture, shape sensing, channel estimation and an optimiser. The flexible microwave metasurface has the aperture, the shape sensors, the inference engine and the controller, and no channel estimator; its loop closes on geometry [7]. The flexible active array has the aperture, the sensing and the closed-loop optimisation, and its loop closes on a beacon rather than on a communication channel [23]. Those two hold three of the four each, and they are the closest any reviewed platform comes. The mechanical platforms hold fewer: the filamentary mesh senses its shape and optimises against it, and has no radio, so it holds two [22]; the soft surface is actuated from a stored script and neither senses nor optimises, so it holds none [8]. What those two contribute is the component the other pair lacks, and it is not a member of the set at all — a geometry the system commands. No platform holds all four, and no platform holds three of them together with a commanded geometry. That is an integration gap rather than a measurement gap, and it explains why the timing gap has persisted: no platform in the reviewed evidence base is the object whose timing would need measuring.

None of this shows that the missing intervals are large, or that the architecture is infeasible. It shows that within the reviewed set they have not been measured. These intervals remain unquantified; the review does not show that they are too large. The narrower claim the review does support is that in the specific feasibility claims examined here, the confidence expressed exceeds the evidence traced behind it.
