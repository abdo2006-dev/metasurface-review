## 6. Implications and research priorities

### 6.1 A reporting set derived from the gaps

The deficiencies documented in Sections 4 and 5 are principally failures of *definition* rather than of measurement quality. Several of the values in Table 2 are excellent measurements; what they lack is the context that would allow them to be compared with anything, or that would allow a downstream reader to detect a change of scope. Once such a value enters a citation chain, an element-level figure can become a surface-level one with nothing on the page to signal it.

The set below therefore prescribes definitions rather than thresholds. We do not propose that actuation should complete within any particular time, that settling should meet any particular criterion value, or that any performance target should be met. The evidence supports no numerical acceptance threshold, and proposing one would introduce exactly the kind of unsupported figure this review documents. Each field is motivated by a recurring reporting deficiency or a specific unresolved quantity in the reviewed set, and those are different things: some fields name quantities no reviewed source reports at all, others name quantities that some sources report and others omit, which is what makes the reported ones incomparable.

**Table 5 — Minimum reporting fields for a deformable or reconfigurable aperture.** The ten fields whose absence caused the specific failures documented in this review. The full checklist, with the observed deficiency motivating every field, is Supplementary Table S4.

| # | Field | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Object timed** — one element, one tile, or the whole aperture | One mechanical platform's element and full-surface figures differ by a factor of ten; quoted without the object, either can stand for the other, and this review found an element figure tabulated downstream as a surface one |
| 2 | **Physical start event**, named | "Response time" denotes at least five different intervals in the reviewed set; one panel's two reported bounds, on the same object, differ by two orders of magnitude with no start event stated for either |
| 3 | **Physical end event**, named | Most reported intervals state no end criterion at all |
| 4 | **Threshold or criterion** defining that end event | The two sources that state one state incommensurable ones: 10 %/90 % amplitude thresholds, and the first video frame showing no visible deviation on a 60 fps camera |
| 5 | **Measured, simulated, projected or derived** | A projected sub-2 ms figure appears in the same discussion as measured 15 ms and 72 ms values |
| 6 | **Actuation and settling reported separately** | Bundled into a single "response time" wherever both are reported |
| 7 | **Deformation source: externally imposed or commanded** | The distinction between compensating a shape and commanding one is the central taxonomic finding here, and is rarely stated explicitly |
| 8 | **Whether a radio-frequency layer is present**, and what it consists of | Every measured mechanical timescale in the reviewed set was obtained on a platform without one |
| 9 | **Calibration procedure and its measured duration** | The one demonstrated post-deformation recalibration of a large flexible aperture reports no time |
| 10 | **Criterion for trustworthy RF operation, and the interval from command to meeting it** | Measured for static-geometry electronic compensation, so the end event is definable and instrumentable; unreported after a commanded geometry change |

Two further groups belong in any full report and are set out in the supplement: the mechanical fields — actuation mechanism and drive level, displacement in millimetres *and* in wavelengths, shape accuracy against the *commanded* shape, repeatability and cycles to a stated electromagnetic degradation, repetition count and dispersion for every reported interval, and actuation energy per shape change — and the control-chain fields, covering interface type and its measured transport contribution, serialisation or multiplexing penalty, computation platform and wall-clock inference time, and whether the loop is open or closed and on what quantity it closes.

The system literature cannot be expected to measure hardware. It can reasonably be expected to declare what it assumes, and the reviewed papers largely do not. Such a declaration should state which adaptation stages are assumed instantaneous — in all four reviewed FIM system papers, control transport, actuation, settling and calibration are assumed away and none says so; the assumed update rate for each control variable, and whether a two-rate schedule is chosen for tractability or justified by hardware; the assumed validity duration of a computed shape, with a bound, a condition, or an explicit acknowledgement that none is claimed; whether the cited hardware has a radio-frequency layer; and the provenance of any hardware figure imported into the paper, meaning what object it was measured on and what its start and end events were. The last item is the most consequential: a table of imported hardware parameters carrying an "object measured" column and a "start/end events" column would have made the traceability audit of §5.2 unnecessary, and the scope change documented there would have been explicit on the page.

We do not propose this as a standard. We propose it as the minimum set that would have allowed the questions in this review to be answered from the published record. Some of its fields could be reported from measurements already performed; others require new ones.

### 6.2 What follows for system modelling

Three consequences, in descending order of confidence. The first is to report the object: the most common failure observed here is not optimism but underspecification, and a latency without an object is uninterpretable. System papers importing hardware figures should import the object with them.

The second is not to assume the electronic layer is uniformly fast. Electronic update latency is mechanism-dependent: the reviewed liquid-crystal device exhibits tens-of-milliseconds transitions at the layer thickness measured, whereas semiconductor control reports different quantities at different granularities, neither of them a material transition time. Whether either mechanism satisfies a particular fast-loop requirement depends on the required state-validity interval and on the architecture, and no reviewed source establishes a scenario-specific deadline against which to judge.

The third is to model the control network. Aperture-scale command distribution can be a major contributor to update latency — a stated factor-of-four serialisation penalty on one measured panel, roughly a third of the measured loop on the other — and it is absent from the FIM system models reviewed here. We claim no dominance, since the two platforms do not agree closely enough to support one and neither reports the decomposition that would settle it; the implication survives either reading, because it concerns whether the cost is modelled at all rather than how large it is. The reviewed system papers simulate 4 to 16 movable elements while the measured hardware has 32, 256 or 512 controllable channels, so a cost that scales with channel count is invisible at the scale the theory currently explores, and the theory's element counts are more likely to grow than shrink as apertures scale.

### 6.3 The highest-value missing measurements

The gap is narrow enough to name three experiments. They are ordered below by how much new hardware each appears to require, which is a judgement about experimental prerequisites and not the result of a cost study; we have performed none.

**Time an experiment that already exists.** G3 appears to require the least new hardware, because the apparatus has already been assembled once: closed-loop recalibration of a deformed 256-element flexible active array is demonstrated but not timed. If that platform remains available, adding synchronised timing from the curvature change to the re-converged focus, at a stated performance tolerance, would convert the reviewed set's strongest qualitative demonstration into a quantitative constraint without a new aperture prototype. Whether the apparatus is still available, whether the deformation and the radio-frequency measurement can be triggered against a common clock, and how reproducible the interval proves to be are unknown to us.

**Instrument a flexible reflective aperture mechanically.** G1 and G2 could be investigated by integrating already-demonstrated classes of flexible radio-frequency apertures and actuation mechanisms in one instrumented experiment, measuring displacement, settling against a stated and justified electromagnetic criterion, and the resulting reflection-phase transient. We prescribe no particular fraction of a wavelength; choosing and justifying one is part of the experiment. Both the apertures and the actuation mechanisms exist separately; integrating actuation with a radiating flexible stack is itself an engineering problem, and within the reviewed set the combination has not been built and timed. It would also settle whether the element-level figures now in circulation bear any relation to a radiating aperture. G4 follows from these and requires a radio-frequency measurement synchronised to the actuation command, which is the natural output of the same experiment.

**Close a loop on a channel.** The integration gap is a research programme rather than an experiment. It requires a platform that senses geometry, estimates a channel, optimises both geometry and phase, actuates, and measures the resulting link. Nothing in the reviewed literature is close to it, and no timeline should be asserted for it.

### 6.4 Limitations

The search was not systematic. Sources were assembled by convenience and supplemented by a documented external search, with no protocol-driven indexed-database queries and no second screener. The forward citation search of §5.2 is the exception in being reproducible, and it too is bounded: 13 of the 20 relevant system studies were obtainable in full text and 7 are behind publisher paywalls, so the practice could extend into papers we could not read, and every count reported is a lower bound.

No reviewed source reports uncertainty on a headline timing value. Neither supplementary package states a repetition count, a sample size or a dispersion figure for its principal interval, although one subordinate quantity is stated as 0.35 ± 0.15 s, which shows the deficiency is real rather than assumed: those authors did report dispersion where they had it. The headline intervals are single stated figures.

Three sources were readable only as page images, their metadata and abstracts read from rendered pages, and no load-bearing claim is drawn from them. Some sources remain identified bibliographically only, and quantitative claims keyed to them should be re-verified against their published versions before submission.

Extraction and classification were performed by a single reviewer and were not independently duplicated or adjudicated. Architecture assignment, stage coding, the timing-status judgements and evidence-level assignment each rest on one reader's judgement, with no second screener and no disagreement-resolution procedure. The boundaries most exposed to this are "assumed" against "unclear", and the distinction between a timed interval and one marked as incompletely defined. A duplicated extraction is the single methodological improvement most likely to change a cell.

The reviewed set is small. Twenty-four distinct contributions is enough to support the specific traceability and definitional findings, and not enough to support any statistical statement about the literature; we make none.
