## 3. FIM architectures and adaptation timescales

### 3.1 Architecture classes, and what may transfer between them

Evidence about FIM feasibility is drawn from seven physically distinct kinds of system, and a statement true of one is routinely false of another. A response time measured on a filamentary mechanical mesh, an update rate measured on a rigid PIN-diode panel and a compensation loop measured on a bending varactor-loaded sheet are not three data points describing one technology; they describe three technologies whose measurements are nonetheless quoted in support of one architecture. The classes are therefore not seven things called flexible metasurfaces. Only A1 through A4 are named as flexible or morphing metasurfaces by the literature that uses them, and even there the naming is inconsistent; A5 to A7 appear because the FIM literature draws evidence from them, not because their authors claim the label. Distinguishing them is what allows the transfer rules at the end of this subsection to be stated.

Three questions distinguish the classes: what physically changes, whether the change is imposed from outside or commanded by the system, and whether the radio-frequency state is independently programmable. Table 1 answers them alongside the questions that decide what a measurement is worth.

**Table 1 — Architecture classes drawn on by the FIM evidence base.** A1–A4 are the direct classes; A5–A7 are comparator or evidence-donor classes whose authors do not generally use the FIM label.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Theoretical FIM array with independently movable elements | element coordinates | an optimisation variable — no mechanism specified | in some formulations | ✗ — no hardware | simulated only | [1], [2], [3], [15] |
| **A2** | Flexible **passive** reflective metasurface | sheet curvature | external | ✗ | ✓ at fixed curvatures | ✗ | [16], [17] |

No source in the reviewed set demonstrates A1 hardware. The FIM system papers define the surface as a coordinate vector constrained to a morphing range and entering the array response through the propagation phase [1, Eqs. (1a)–(3), p. 13321]; they specify no actuator, no sheet mechanics, no inter-element mechanical coupling and no fabrication route. Where physical plausibility is argued, two mechanical platforms are cited: a liquid-metal microfluidic network in an elastomeric matrix, and an array of metallic filaments actuated by distributed Lorentz forces [1, p. 13320], citing [8] and [7] respectively. That is a plausibility argument and should be read as one. Both cited platforms are A4, and carry no meta-atoms, no bias network, no dielectric substrate and no radio-frequency ground plane. When a flexible reflective microwave surface was actually built, achieving mechanical flexibility required replacing a solid copper ground with a serpentine mesh, a change reported to reduce estimated bending stiffness by more than two orders of magnitude [6, p. 3].

The two realised surfaces in the reviewed set that are simultaneously flexible, reflective and electronically programmable are A3, and they share a property that the A1 formulation reverses. In the flexible microwave metasurface with shape-guided adaptive programming [6], a 480 × 240 mm sheet carrying 32 × 16 meta-atoms is bent by an external mechanical platform, a conformal array of 32 strain sensors reconstructs the resulting shape, and a trained neural network maps that shape to the column bias voltages that restore the intended electromagnetic behaviour. In the conformal reconfigurable reflectarray built from polydimethylsiloxane and printed liquid metal [18], a 10 × 10 array at 9 GHz with per-element PIN diodes achieves ±45° beam scanning using element-level conformal compensation phase, mounted on acrylic supports of different bending degrees, so each curvature is a separate static experiment and the surface neither senses nor commands its shape. In both cases geometry is a disturbance to be compensated. In the A1 formulation geometry is the *primary control variable* — the thing chosen to maximise an objective — and the channel is what is being tracked. Within the reviewed set, no source demonstrates that inversion in hardware.

The two A4 platforms supply every measured mechanical timescale in this review, and neither has an electromagnetic function. The filamentary mechanical metasurface [7] is a 4 × 4 mesh of thin metal and polyimide traces on an 18.0 mm sample, driven by distributed Lorentz forces, and uniquely in the reviewed set it closes a loop on shape, with in-situ stereo imaging feeding an experiment-driven inverse design. The soft shape-programmable surface [8] uses liquid-metal microfluidic ribbons in an elastomer, and can fix a programmed shape through a liquid-metal phase transition, a mechanism by which a slowly-set geometry could in principle be held without continuous actuation. These are the right platforms to cite for mechanical feasibility and the wrong platforms to cite for a FIM response time, a distinction Section 5 shows has not always been maintained.

Two adjacent bodies of work bound the problem from outside. A large conformal literature concerns apertures whose shape is known and does not change, and establishes that validated design methods and measured hardware exist for important fixed-curvature cases across several bands, phase resolutions and synthesis approaches — integral-equation synthesis accounting for finite size, coupling and spatial dispersion [22]; a reduced-order coupled-dipole model of a cylindrically conformal waveguide-fed metasurface [23]; an active conformal metasurface lens [24]; cylindrical RIS design carried from surface-impedance synthesis to a one-bit implementation [25]; a 3D-printed curved two-bit coding surface [26]; and a flexible liquid-metal-on-PDMS reflectarray holding its radiation characteristics across a ±25 % bending range at a fixed coding pattern [17]. That is prior art rather than closure, and it is prior art about a different problem: curvature is a design parameter there, not a control variable. Separately, flexible active antenna arrays (A7) supply the strongest evidence that large flexible coherent apertures are realisable, with two 256-element, 30 × 30 cm arrays remaining fully functional and programmable at concave and convex bend radii below 23 cm under closed-loop focusing driven by feedback from a remote receiver, and calibration explicitly targeting phase offsets caused by manufacturing variation, shape deformation and environmental change [20].

Four transfer rules follow, and are applied without exception in the remainder of the manuscript. A4 timing does not transfer to A1 or A3 without qualification, because A4 platforms have no radio-frequency layer and their measured response times bound a different mechanical object. An A5 electronic switching time is not a mechanical settling time. An A3 partial-loop latency is not a full-loop latency. And A7 is not A5: an active array's calibration architecture, which senses through its own transmit chain and closes a loop against a remote beacon, does not carry over to a passive reflective aperture, which has neither.

Figure 1 draws the consequence. Its two axes are properties of an experiment — has the platform demonstrated commanded physical geometry, and has radio-frequency performance been measured on it — so only platforms that performed an experiment are plotted; a modelling paper answers neither question, and placing simulation work in a hardware quadrant would be a category error. Three of the four cells are populated: the commanded-geometry-without-measured-RF cell by the two A4 mechanical platforms [7, 8], and the measured-RF-without-commanded-geometry cell by every A2, A3, A5 and A7 platform in the reviewed set [4, 5, 6, 16, 17, 18, 20, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. The cell requiring commanded geometry *and* measured radio-frequency performance together is empty. A1 does not occupy a cell in that map: it names the empty one, because the A1 system model is the specification of an operating point — geometry chosen by the optimiser, on an aperture whose radiation is what the optimisation is for — rather than physical evidence to be classified.

### 3.2 The adaptation chain

FIM adaptation names a sequence, not an event, and the sequence crosses three physical domains — perception, computation and mechanics — which have no reason to share a magnitude. Decomposing it is what makes it possible to say *which process a given number times*, and it is what turns an apparently contradictory set of reported latencies into a consistent picture.

| Stage | | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Sensing / acquisition | physical observation of the surface or environment — strain sensors, imaging, received pilots |
| S2 | Geometry estimation | conversion of raw sensor data into a surface-shape estimate |
| S3 | Channel estimation | acquisition of instantaneous or statistical channel state |
| S4 | Optimisation / inference | computation of the desired geometry and phase configuration |
| S5 | Control transmission | transport of the command from the compute element to the surface controller |
| S6 | Electronic state update | meta-atom electromagnetic state change |
| S7 | Mechanical morphing | the commanded physical displacement |
| S8 | Mechanical settling | decay of transient motion to within tolerance of the target shape |
| S9 | Calibration | re-establishment of the geometry-to-RF-response mapping after the shape changed |
| S10 | Stabilised RF operation | the interval over which the configured surface delivers its intended behaviour |

Across the four reviewed FIM system papers — three distinct studies, since one is a short version of another — stages S5 through S9 are absent from the model or assumed instantaneous. Geometry takes the value the optimiser assigns it, at the moment the optimiser assigns it. Section 4 records what has been measured for each stage, and Supplementary Section S6 gives the per-source coded matrix from which those counts are taken.

### 3.3 What the surface is being asked to track

Treating "the channel" as one clock obscures that a FIM-assisted ISAC system tracks several states with different rates and different consequences for failure: instantaneous complex fading, driven by Doppler; channel statistics and spatial correlation, deliberately treated as slower; angles of arrival and departure, which follow the user's traverse across the cell rather than the fading; target kinematics in the sensing subproblem; blockage, which is event-like rather than periodic; the geometry of the aperture itself; and the calibration state that determines when the aperture may be trusted again after a shape change. High-mobility models update angles explicitly and separately from fading correlation [19], and communication and sensing may impose different movement and update requirements [11, pp. 24–25]. The reviewed set fixes no relative rates among these states, and none of them carries an update deadline, because assigning one requires a performance tolerance the reviewed sources do not supply. Supplementary Table S7 records each state with its driver, the control variable it binds, and the evidence available for it.

The load-bearing question is which of these states drives the shape optimisation. It matters that the slow-geometry reading is not this review's reconstruction. The version of record of the high-mobility FIM–ISAC study states both halves of the trade-off explicitly: distributed Lorentz forces "enable rapid reconfiguration of the FIM surface shape within millisecond switching speeds", "comparable to typical channel coherence times in high-mobility wireless scenarios" [1, p. 13321]; and "the FIM surface shape can also be morphed once over several channel coherence intervals to achieve statistically optimal performance, thus offering a flexible trade-off between achievable performance gains and morphing speed requirements" [1, p. 13322]. The two halves pull in opposite directions and both should be taken at face value. The second establishes that operating the geometry on a slower schedule than the fading is a design option the system literature already permits, so the conditional framing this review adopts in Section 5 is a premise supplied by that literature rather than one imposed on it. The first is an assertion about another paper's hardware, and Section 5 traces it. A further FIM paper makes the fast reading more precise and equally untested: FIM surface shapes "are only updated on the timescale of the channel's coherence block" [29]. That is a legitimate modelling choice, and it asserts that command transport, actuation, settling and any recalibration all complete inside one coherence block.

Two costs are subtracted from whatever budget applies before any hardware acts. Pilot overhead scales with the controlled element count: the ON/OFF estimation protocol for a passive RIS requires 1 + M time slots to estimate the direct link and M reflected links sequentially [19, p. 720], while the FIM case acquires a two-dimensional measurement of Q subframes each containing T₂ time slots, with the element moving once per subframe and the phase adjusted once per time slot [3, p. 6829]. These are protocol units and we do not convert them to milliseconds, since the sources supply no numerology; but the overhead scales with the very quantity that makes a FIM attractive, and the FIM estimation protocol already contains a two-rate schedule, so the hierarchical structure a system architect might propose as a solution is present as a modelling premise before any hardware has been asked whether it can meet either rate. Computation is likewise not free. One reviewed FIM paper reports wall-clock computation time, and it is the only such measurement on a FIM-specific algorithm in the reviewed set: a clustering mean-field variational sparse Bayesian learning estimator on a stated desktop processor rises from roughly 4 ms for a small virtual array to roughly 100 ms at a virtual array size of 324, with the slowest benchmark reaching about 1.2 s [3, Fig. 8, p. 6833].⁴ It bounds nothing for an embedded implementation, but it *is* a measurement, which distinguishes it from the iteration counts the other FIM papers report in place of durations, and it scales with virtual array size: the computation that makes element movement useful becomes more expensive precisely as the movement becomes more ambitious.
