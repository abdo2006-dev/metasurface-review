## 1. Introduction

A reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) changes how it scatters by changing the electromagnetic state of its meta-atoms. A flexible intelligent metasurface (FIM) changes how it scatters by changing where its elements are. In the system-level formulation, each radiating element carries a coordinate along the surface normal; the vector of those coordinates defines the surface shape; the shape enters the array response through the propagation phase; and it is then optimised jointly with beamforming or sensing objectives, subject to a morphing range [1, Eqs. (1a)–(3), p. 13321; 2, Eq. (1)]. Simulated gains are reported across several formulations: a transmit-power reduction of about 3 dB at a one-wavelength morphing range for a multiuser downlink [2], further gains from introducing and then optimising a surface in a doubly dispersive multiple-input multiple-output ISAC setting [1, p. 13328], and element movement reported as more effective than passive phase control at enhancing received signal strength in a single-input single-output multipath scenario, with joint optimisation better still [3, p. 6823].

What distinguishes geometry control from electronic state control is that exercising it requires moving matter. An electromagnetic state change propagates at the speed of a tuning mechanism; a geometry change propagates at the speed of an actuator, a structure and its damping. The system models do not represent that difference, and are not obliged to: in all four FIM system papers reviewed here the shape is a variable that may be set, and the mechanism that sets it lies outside the model.

In quasi-static conditions, idealising the actuation may be less consequential for system-level performance. Under high mobility its timing assumptions become potentially load-bearing, because the state the surface was configured for goes on changing while the surface responds. At the operating point of the high-mobility FIM–ISAC study reviewed here, 28 GHz and a maximum velocity of 208 m/s, the maximum Doppler shift is approximately 19.4 kHz.¹ It is the combination of a millimetre-wave carrier with a velocity of that order that places the system outside the conditions under which the reviewed flexible and reconfigurable-surface hardware has been characterised: those demonstrations span a wide range of carriers, but none is characterised under mobility of this kind.

Three quantities must be kept apart here, and they are easily conflated. A Doppler frequency follows from carrier and velocity, and 19.4 kHz is exact given the stated parameters. A coherence interval is a threshold-dependent correlation statistic whose threshold is a choice, and the consequence of that choice is measurable: in a RIS-assisted high-mobility study at 2.6 GHz with a user moving at 90 mph, the interval over which the time-correlation function exceeds 0.5 covers 51 symbols, while the same paper's bit-error-rate comparison uses 20 symbol periods at 0.95 correlation and 40 at 0.82 [19, p. 721 and Fig. 4(b), p. 728]: one scenario, three answers spanning a factor of 2.5. An update deadline requires in addition a performance tolerance that somebody has selected, and this review defines none. What the Doppler figure establishes on its own is a regime, strongly time-selective, rather than an interval. The question that remains is therefore not whether the surface is fast enough, which cannot be answered without a deadline, but which operations in the loop are bound to the fast-varying state at all.

A natural next step is to ask how quickly a flexible metasurface reconfigures. The reviewed literature reports several such intervals, but they do not describe the same quantity. A scalable one-bit PIN-diode RIS reports an update time below 0.1 ms in its abstract and a tile reconfiguration below 10 ms in its results section [4]; both are correct, both bound the same 16 × 16 tile, and neither ends at a stated electromagnetic criterion, so the two orders of magnitude between them record a change of context rather than a change of speed. A liquid-crystal RIS reports approximately 15 ms and 72 ms, which are switch-on and switch-off transitions of a 4.6 μm material layer between 10 % and 90 % thresholds [5]. A flexible microwave metasurface reports 16.76 ms for an interval running from shape acquisition to bias-voltage supply, which contains neither the deformation that produced the shape nor any settling afterwards [6]. A soft shape-programmable surface reports approximately 30 ms for an isolated ribbon against approximately 300 ms for a full surface developing from flat [8]. These are not competing estimates of one quantity. They are measurements of different quantities, on different architectures, with different definitions of when the clock starts and stops, and quoting any one of them as *the* FIM response time yields a figure with no defined referent. Separating them, and asking what survives the separation, is the task this review undertakes.

Three broad reviews in the reviewed set border this territory and partition it cleanly enough to leave a specific hole. Saifullah *et al.* classify tunable metasurfaces by tuning mechanism and rank their speeds qualitatively, with electrical tuning preferred above kilohertz modulation rates, liquid crystal below about 1 kHz, microfluidic tuning on a millisecond scale, and mechanically stretchable substrates slow, environmentally sensitive and unable to address individual unit cells. There is no discussion of ISAC [9]. Tishchenko *et al.* survey multi-functional and hybrid RIS for ISAC, treat controller and network latency as a practical constraint, and link higher carrier frequencies to shorter coherence times, with no discussion of mechanical tuning [10]. Ma *et al.* survey reconfigurable and movable antennas and state the position closest to ours: mechanical movement is generally slower than electronic reconfiguration; instantaneous-CSI position optimisation suits quasi-static environments with long coherence times, while fast fading calls for statistical-CSI approaches that reduce movement frequency; and hierarchical cross-layer control, in which statistical information guides slow movement while rapid state changes are decoupled, is an open direction [11, pp. 3, 20, 26–27]. The same survey argues that future models should incorporate inertia, settling time, friction, backlash, movement accuracy and energy rather than idealised instantaneous positioning.

Two consequences follow, and the first constrains what this manuscript may claim. The slow-geometry, fast-electronics division of labour is established prior art rather than a novelty available to this review or to any FIM paper: statistical-CSI FIM optimisation has been published [12, 13], a two-rate schedule of element movement per subframe and phase adjustment per time slot is already embedded in a FIM channel-estimation protocol [3, p. 6829], and delay-aware modelling of reconfigurable apertures has begun in the adjacent fluid-antenna literature [14]. The second consequence is that none of the three reviews treats FIMs as a dedicated architecture under those terms: a full-text search of each returns no occurrence of "FIM" or "flexible intelligent metasurface", which establishes the absence of explicit treatment under that label rather than the absence of conceptually related coverage.² Ma *et al.* recommend that models include movement time, settling and energy; no reviewed source establishes which of those quantities have been measured, on what object, or under what definition.

That is the gap this review addresses, and the question it asks is:

> Which operations in a FIM-assisted high-mobility ISAC system must track fast channel variation, which may follow slower geometric or statistical change, and how well are the resulting timescales supported by existing hardware and control demonstrations?

We answer it as an evidence-mapping problem rather than a design problem, and within the literature reviewed here and the searches recorded in Section 2 the contributions are four.

**C1 — An architecture-aware, stage-resolved adaptation-chain framework.** FIM adaptation is decomposed into ten stages from sensing to stabilised radiation, and each source's treatment of each stage is coded on two axes: what the source establishes about the stage, and what it establishes about how long the stage takes. Applied to the reviewed set, no platform measures the duration of more than four of the ten stages, and the platform reaching four has no radio-frequency layer.

**C2 — A definition-preserving timing register.** Every reported value is recorded with its timed object, start event, end event and measurement status, and values may be compared only within a comparability class. Two common simplifications do not survive that discipline: an element-level update time cannot stand in for an aperture-level configuration time, as one panel's own two bounds show; and the reported ranges for electronic and mechanical processes overlap once the tuning mechanism is named, so an architecture label alone does not order them.

**C3 — A traceability audit of the hardware-feasibility premises in the FIM system literature.** Three premises are traced to the primary measurements behind them. Among the 13 of 20 candidate citing studies whose full texts were accessible, four reuse a timing value with its scope changed, and those four form one connected co-authorship network; the remainder cite the same hardware and attach no timing value at all. Every count is a lower bound on a partially observed set, and no prevalence claim is made. Section 5.2 gives the instances and the bounds.

**C4 — A statement of the validation gap and a reporting set that addresses it.** Four intervals are unmeasured in the reviewed set, and the reporting fields we propose prescribe definitions — object, start event, end event — rather than acceptance thresholds.

We propose no hardware, no algorithm and no control architecture. The manuscript is an audit, and it rests on the accuracy of its citations.
